Using symmetries ​

In Your first ground state and The thermodynamic limit we treated the transverse-field Ising model (TFIM) as a generic spin chain. But the TFIM is not generic: it has a symmetry, and in this tutorial we teach MPSKit about it.

Recall the Hamiltonian,

and consider the global spin flip  , which flips every spin at once. Conjugating by sends   , so the interaction term picks up two minus signs and is unchanged, while the field term commutes with trivially. Hence commutes with . Since  , this is a symmetry, and every eigenstate of can be labelled by a parity quantum number: even (  ) or odd (  ).

MPSKit, through the TensorKit tensor backend, can bake this symmetry directly into the tensors of the MPS. Doing so buys you two things. First, the tensors become block-sparse: at the same total bond dimension the computer multiplies smaller dense blocks, which is faster. Second, every state you compute carries an explicit sector label, so "the lowest odd-parity excitation" becomes something you can ask for directly. This tutorial demonstrates both, by redoing the finite-chain TFIM calculation once without and once with the symmetry.

Why \\mathbb{Z}_2 and not U(1)?

   is the symmetry the TFIM actually has, and it is what MPSKitModels.transverse_field_ising supports: its symmetry argument accepts Trivial, Z2Irrep or FermionParity, and anything else — U1Irrep included — throws an ArgumentError. Larger groups pay off more, but need a model that has them: for U(1) see Constructing states, and for SU(2) the Heisenberg pages in the examples gallery.

Loading the packages ​

Every code block on this page shares one Julia session, so we load the packages once. Z2Irrep and Z2Space, the symmetry-aware building blocks used below, come from TensorKit.

julia
using MPSKit, MPSKitModels, TensorKit

1. Recap: the ground state without symmetry ​

We start from the workflow of the first tutorial: a chain of L = 16 sites, a random FiniteMPS, and a DMRG ground-state search. Two small changes from before: we set the field to g = 2.0, and we use a total bond dimension of 16.

Why g = 2.0? This puts us deep in the paramagnetic phase, where the ground state respects the spin-flip symmetry. That matters for what comes next: an MPS built from symmetric tensors lives in exactly one parity sector and cannot spontaneously break the symmetry, so a fair comparison needs a point where the true ground state is symmetric to begin with.

julia
L = 16
H = transverse_field_ising(FiniteChain(L); g = 2.0)
ψ₀ = FiniteMPS(L, ℂ^2, ℂ^16)
ψ, envs, ϵ = find_groundstate(ψ₀, H, DMRG(; verbosity = 0))
E = expectation_value(ψ, H)
-33.90185203448317 - 1.3942483812553657e-16im

This is our reference number: the ground-state energy computed with plain, symmetry-oblivious tensors.

2. The same model, with the symmetry made explicit ​

To exploit the symmetry we change two lines: the Hamiltonian and the initial state.

For the Hamiltonian, we pass the symmetry as an extra first argument. transverse_field_ising(Z2Irrep, ...) builds the same Hamiltonian as before, but out of tensors that manifestly commute with the spin flip:

H_Z2 = transverse_field_ising(Z2Irrep, FiniteChain(L); g = 2.0)

For the state, the plain spaces ℂ^2 and ℂ^16 are replaced by graded spaces that keep track of parity:

julia
ψ₀_Z2 = FiniteMPS(L, Z2Space(0 => 1, 1 => 1), Z2Space(0 => 8, 1 => 8))

The syntax reads as a list of sector => dimension pairs, where sector 0 is the even (  ) irrep of and sector 1 is the odd (  ) one:

  • The physical space Z2Space(0 => 1, 1 => 1) is the familiar two-dimensional spin-1/2 site, now split into its symmetry content: one even state and one odd state.

  • The virtual space Z2Space(0 => 8, 1 => 8) says the bond carries 8 states of even parity and 8 of odd parity — 16 in total, matching the ℂ^16 of the plain run, so the two calculations have exactly the same variational power.

From here the workflow is unchanged:

julia
ψ_Z2, envs_Z2, ϵ_Z2 = find_groundstate(ψ₀_Z2, H_Z2, DMRG(; verbosity = 0))
E_Z2 = expectation_value(ψ_Z2, H_Z2)
-33.90185203448316 + 3.5127272400147646e-15im

Both runs found the same ground state, and the two energies agree to numerical precision:

julia
E, E_Z2
(-33.90185203448317 - 1.3942483812553657e-16im, -33.90185203448316 + 3.5127272400147646e-15im)

Same physics, different bookkeeping

Nothing about the model changed — only the way its tensors are stored. The symmetric calculation restricts the search to states of definite (here: even) parity, and stores only the tensor blocks the symmetry allows to be nonzero.

3. The payoff, part 1: block-sparse tensors ​

Where did the symmetry go? Into the structure of the state. Ask for the virtual space at the central bond and you no longer get an anonymous ℂ^16, but a space that knows its sector decomposition:

julia
V = left_virtualspace(ψ_Z2, L ÷ 2)
Rep[ℤ₂](…) of dim 16:
 0 => 8
 1 => 8

Its total dimension is still 16:

julia
dim(V)
16

The same sector labels show up in every quantity derived from the state. The entanglement spectrum at the central cut, for instance, now comes back resolved by sector — compare Entanglement entropy and spectrum, where the same call on an unsymmetric state produced a single Trivial() block:

julia
spectrum = entanglement_spectrum(ψ_Z2, L ÷ 2)
collect(keys(spectrum))
2-element Vector{TensorKitSectors.Z2Irrep}:
 0
 1

Iterating pairs gives each sector together with its singular values:

julia
collect(pairs(spectrum))
2-element Vector{Pair{TensorKitSectors.Z2Irrep, SubArray{Float64, 1, Vector{Float64}, Tuple{UnitRange{Int64}}, true}}}:
 0 => [0.9911302803407792, 0.00032012391475386, 5.743506568362074e-6, 1.0321887506671106e-7, 1.0067975291036678e-7, 1.809063049766392e-9, 1.521808453420011e-9, 3.077819966084831e-11]
 1 => [0.13287197219118196, 0.002387896409916173, 4.284246843595354e-5, 7.509995430430776e-7, 1.3837630512317706e-8, 1.1354558185544535e-8, 2.425021392934755e-10, 1.0703730487159722e-10]

This block structure is where the speedup comes from: instead of multiplying one dense 16-dimensional bond index, the computer multiplies two independent blocks of roughly half that size, and the forbidden matrix elements between the sectors are never stored or touched at all. At bond dimension 16 the difference is negligible, but the saving grows with the bond dimension and with the size of the symmetry group.

4. The payoff, part 2: sectors label the physics ​

The sector labels are not just an implementation detail — they classify the eigenstates of , and MPSKit lets you target a sector directly.

In the paramagnetic phase the lowest excitation of the TFIM is, roughly speaking, a single flipped spin. Flipping one spin changes the parity of the state, so this excitation lives in the odd sector — a different sector than the (even) ground state.

The excitations function computes excited states on top of a converged ground state; on a finite chain it takes the Hamiltonian, an algorithm, the ground state, and its environments, and returns energies measured above the ground state. By default it searches the trivial (even) sector:

julia
Es_even, ϕs_even = excitations(H_Z2, QuasiparticleAnsatz(), ψ_Z2, envs_Z2; num = 1)
Es_even[1]
4.2908540838578695

The sector keyword redirects the search to the odd sector:

julia
Es_odd, ϕs_odd = excitations(
    H_Z2, QuasiparticleAnsatz(), ψ_Z2, envs_Z2;
    num = 1, sector = Z2Irrep(1)
)
Es_odd[1]
2.060065807417826

The odd-sector excitation is indeed the lower one:

julia
Es_odd[1] < Es_even[1]
true

Without the symmetry built into the tensors, this question could not even be posed: the plain calculation of Section 1 has no notion of parity to select on. More ways to use excitations — dispersion relations, other algorithms, infinite chains — are collected in Excited states.

Where to go next ​

You have run the flagship TFIM calculation with its symmetry made explicit: the same physics at the same total bond dimension, but with block-sparse tensors and sector labels on everything the calculation produces.

The same syntax scales up to larger symmetry groups, where the payoff grows. For a U(1) symmetry (particle number, magnetization) the graded spaces list integer or half-integer charges instead of parities — worked constructions are in Constructing states, Section 9. For non-abelian symmetries such as SU(2) the gains are more dramatic still, because each symmetric block then represents an entire multiplet of states; the spin-1 Haldane chain and XXZ Heisenberg pages in the examples gallery show this in action.

To continue the tutorial track, Quasiparticle excitations develops the excitation calculation of Section 4 into a full dispersion relation; the recipe collection for excited states is Excited states, and the one for building symmetric states is Constructing states.
